Five questions before drawing a product

  1. Read: Identify carbon skeleton, functional group, bonds, charges and any leaving group.
  2. Reagent: Is the chemical acting as an acid, base, nucleophile, electrophile, oxidant or reductant?
  3. Pathway: What bond is broken, what new bond forms, and is a carbocation or bridged ion possible?
  4. Electrons: Start each curved arrow at a lone pair or bond. End it at an atom or bond where the electrons go. Update charges after each step.
  5. Check: Count carbons and hydrogens, show wedge/dash relationships, and look for rearrangement or a second product.

Speak each decision aloud on a worked example, then try the next molecule on paper without viewing its answer.

Reagent reasoning

Where the atoms go

Common Test 3 confusion: methanol contributes OCH3 after substitution, not OH. TsCl/pyridine converts an alcohol to OTs; chlorine does not replace the alcohol. NaCN can then replace a suitable tosylate through SN2.

Trace-along: A secondary alcohol is treated with TsCl and pyridine, then NaCN in DMSO. Draw the tosylate first. Circle the original carbon-oxygen bond. Next draw cyanide bonded through its carbon, with inversion at the attacked stereocenter.

Reveal teaching check

TsCl changes OH to OTs with retention of configuration at carbon. Cyanide attacks the carbon bearing OTs through SN2, breaks the carbon-oxygen bond, and adds one carbon to the product. If the original C-O bond was a wedge, the new C-CN bond is shown as a dash in the same orientation of the skeleton.

Mechanisms

What happens after the first arrow?

Example: Tertiary alcohol + HBr. Draw these on paper as three separately charged structures: protonated alcohol; carbocation after water leaves; alkyl bromide after Br⁻ attacks.

For each step, annotate: electron source, destination, old bond broken, new bond made, resulting charge.

Show the next-step explanation
  1. O lone pair attacks H of HBr; H-Br electrons go to Br. Oxygen is now positively charged.
  2. C-O electrons go to oxygen; H2O leaves and a carbocation forms.
  3. A Br⁻ lone pair attacks the carbocation; a C-Br bond forms and the charge is neutralized.

For E1 competition, a base removes a beta hydrogen and the C-H bond electrons make a C=C bond instead of Br⁻ adding.

For an exam mechanism, write all participating electron pairs and byproducts. A valid final product alone is not a complete curved-arrow mechanism.

Three-dimensional structures

Do not guess wedges and dashes

With Br2 in an inert solvent, an alkene forms a bromonium ion and undergoes anti addition. With Br2 in water, water can attack the bromonium ion to form a bromohydrin; the OH and Br appear on opposite faces. With H2 and Lindlar's catalyst, an internal alkyne gives a cis alkene by syn addition.

Paper check: Draw cyclopentene and show both enantiomers of its trans dibromide after Br2 in CCl4. Then describe why both bromines cannot be wedges on the same face.

Reveal reasoning

The bromonium bridge occupies one face; Br⁻ opens it from the opposite face. On adjacent carbons, show one wedge and one dash. The two enantiomeric arrangements form a racemic mixture. Be careful: an anti rule recalled correctly but drawn as syn could indicate a drawing slip or a misconception; distinguish by asking for an explanation.

Synthesis

Work backward before choosing reagents

To turn an alkyne into a longer carbon chain, identify the C-C bond to construct. A terminal acetylide formed using NaNH2 can react with a methyl or primary alkyl halide through SN2. A secondary alkyl halide is a poor choice because elimination competes strongly.

Teaching example: Plan a route from ethyne to cis-3-hexene. Use paper and explain why the final hydrogenation must stop at an alkene.

Reveal a possible route

HC≡CH → (1. NaNH2, 2. CH3CH2Br) CH3CH2C≡CH (1-butyne) → (1. NaNH2, 2. CH3CH2Br) CH3CH2C≡CCH2CH3 (3-hexyne) → (H2, Lindlar catalyst) cis-3-hexene.

This worked example is guided study, not new independent mastery evidence.

How to practice today

Use paper first, app second

  1. Do not look at the answer while drawing. Write what you think the reagent does and what happens next.
  2. If stuck, reveal one explanation. Then close it and redraw without looking.
  3. Mark your own confidence: sure, unsure, or guessing. Record the exact first step you cannot explain.
  4. On a later day, solve two different unseen problems without hints. Only the existing learning engine may determine mastery from its properly recorded evidence.
