**Purpose of this file:** teach functional groups as local atom-and-bond patterns that can be found inside unfamiliar molecules. The learner must identify the exact atoms and bonds that justify a classification, distinguish near-lookalikes, recognize that one molecule can contain more than one functional group, and avoid classifying by element presence alone.

---

# 1. LESSON GOAL

A learner who begins with little or no usable functional-group knowledge should leave able to:

1. explain that a functional group is a local pattern of atoms and bonds, not the entire molecule,
2. locate heteroatoms and multiple bonds before naming a pattern,
3. recognize an **alcohol** by a carbon-bonded `O-H` pattern,
4. recognize an **ether** by an oxygen single-bonded between two carbon groups,
5. recognize a **carbonyl** as a `C=O` pattern before classifying carbonyl-containing groups,
6. recognize a **ketone** by a carbonyl carbon bonded to carbon groups on both sides,
7. recognize a **carboxylic acid** by a `C(=O)-O-H` pattern,
8. recognize an **amine** in the current introductory set by a nitrogen that is not directly attached to a carbonyl carbon,
9. recognize an **amide** by a carbonyl carbon directly bonded to nitrogen,
10. distinguish alcohol from ether, ketone from carboxylic acid, and amine from amide using exact connectivity,
11. recognize more than one functional group in the same molecule,
12. reject shortcuts such as `contains O = alcohol` or `contains N = amine`,
13. highlight the exact atoms/bonds that justify a classification,
14. solve a fresh functional-group identification task without prompts.

This lesson is not complete because the learner can match six names to six flashcards. It is complete when she can inspect an unfamiliar structure, isolate the decisive local pattern, and explain why the label fits.

# 2. LEARNER-FACING ORIENTATION

## Screen title

**Functional groups: find the important pattern inside the molecule**

## Dr. Merissa narration

> "A functional group is a small pattern of atoms and bonds inside a molecule. That pattern helps chemists predict how that part of the molecule behaves. We are not going to memorize whole pictures. We are going to learn what exact pattern earns each name."

### Immediate learner check

Visual: show a five-carbon skeleton with one oxygen-containing region highlighted but unnamed.

Prompt:

**If two molecules have different carbon chains but the same important oxygen-bond pattern, could they belong to the same functional-group family?**

Choices:
- Yes, because the local pattern can be the same
- No, the whole molecule must look identical
- I am not sure yet

Correct: **Yes, because the local pattern can be the same**

If missed:

> "The whole molecule can change while the functional-group pattern stays the same. We are going to learn to zoom in on the part that decides the label."

Orientation is instructional only, not mastery evidence.

Do not reteach Lewis structures or full bond-line notation unless the learner actually needs them.

## Gate P1 - atom labels

Show `C`, `O`, and `N` in a simple structure.

Prompt:

**Which symbol is oxygen?**

Correct: `O`

**Which symbol is nitrogen?**

Correct: `N`

If missed, give a tiny symbol repair only.

## Gate P2 - single versus double bond

Show `C-O` and `C=O`.

Prompt:

**Which one contains a double bond?**

Correct: `C=O`

If missed, repair bond-order notation only.

## Gate P3 - carbon positions in bond-line drawings

Show one short bond-line structure with one labeled O.

Prompt:

**Do unlabeled line ends/vertices still represent carbon here?**

Correct: **Yes**

If missed, route to the U1-01 carbon-position micro-repair and return to a fresh gate item.

Once these three ideas are usable, continue. Do not repeat passed gates.

# 4. THE DECISION ROUTINE BEFORE THE NAMES

Teach one reusable routine before six separate labels.

## Routine

When a learner sees an unfamiliar molecule:

1. **Find O and N first.**
2. **Look for any C=O double bond.**
3. **Inspect what is directly attached to those atoms.**
4. **Name the local pattern only after the connectivity is clear.**
5. **Scan again because one molecule may contain more than one functional group.**

### Dr. Merissa narration

> "Functional groups are about who is connected to whom and by what kind of bond. The element symbols matter, but the pattern matters more."

### Low-risk interaction

Show two structures, both containing oxygen:
- one has `C-O-H`,
- one has `C-O-C`.

Prompt:

**Is `contains oxygen` enough information to know the functional group?**

Correct: **No**

Then:

> "Good. The oxygen is the clue. The atoms directly attached to it decide which pattern we have."

# 5. TEACHING SEQUENCE - I DO

## Pair 1 - ALCOHOL versus ETHER

### WATCH STEP 1 - Begin with the oxygen, not the name

Use **1-propanol** in an expanded/bond-line toggle.

Highlight only the oxygen region.

Visual local pattern:
`C-O-H`

Dr. Merissa:

> "This oxygen is bonded to a carbon on one side and a hydrogen on the other. The important local pattern is carbon-oxygen-hydrogen."

Then reveal the term:

**Alcohol**

> "When an -OH group is attached to carbon in the structures we are using here, we call that an alcohol functional group."

### Prediction

Show the same molecule with the carbon chain lengthened while keeping `C-O-H` unchanged.

Prompt:

**Did changing the carbon chain remove the alcohol pattern?**

Correct: **No**

---

### WATCH STEP 2 - Change only one neighbor of oxygen

Use **ethyl methyl ether** as the contrast.

Visual local pattern:
`C-O-C`

Keep oxygen in the same screen position. Replace the O-H bond with O-C.

Dr. Merissa:

> "Now the oxygen is single-bonded to carbon on both sides. That local pattern is different."

Reveal:

**Ether**

> "An ether has an oxygen single-bonded between two carbon groups."

### Interaction

Prompt:

**What single connectivity change turned the alcohol pattern into the ether pattern?**

Supported correct idea:
- O was bonded to H in the alcohol,
- O is bonded to C instead in the ether.

Do not accept `the oxygen moved` if connectivity did not.

---

## Pair 2 - KETONE versus CARBOXYLIC ACID

### WATCH STEP 3 - Introduce carbonyl only when needed

Show a simple `C=O` fragment before naming a functional group.

Dr. Merissa:

> "This carbon is double-bonded to oxygen. Chemists call the C=O unit a **carbonyl**. Carbonyl is a pattern inside several different functional groups, so C=O by itself is not enough to finish the classification."

### Immediate check

Prompt:

**Does every C=O automatically mean ketone?**

Correct: **No**

---

### WATCH STEP 4 - Ketone

Use **2-butanone**.

Highlight:
`C-C(=O)-C`

Dr. Merissa:

> "The carbonyl carbon is bonded to carbon groups on both sides. In this introductory set, that pattern is a ketone."

Reveal:

**Ketone**

### Interaction

Ask learner to tap:
1. the carbonyl carbon,
2. the two carbon neighbors attached to that carbonyl carbon.

If either neighbor is missed, do not advance to the name.

---

### WATCH STEP 5 - Carboxylic acid

Use **propanoic acid**.

Highlight in stages:
1. `C=O`,
2. the same carbonyl carbon bonded to `O-H`,
3. full local pattern `C(=O)-O-H`.

Dr. Merissa:

> "This molecule also has a carbonyl, but the carbonyl carbon is directly bonded to an oxygen that is bonded to hydrogen. That larger pattern changes the functional-group identity."

Reveal:

**Carboxylic acid**

### Prediction

Prompt:

**If I see C=O but the same carbon is also bonded to O-H, should I call the whole pattern a ketone?**

Correct: **No**

Then:

> "Right. The larger local pattern is carboxylic acid."

---

## Pair 3 - AMINE versus AMIDE

### WATCH STEP 6 - Start with nitrogen, then inspect its neighbor

Use **propylamine**.

Highlight the nitrogen and the carbon directly attached to it.

Local pattern for this introductory example:
`C-NH2`

Dr. Merissa:

> "This nitrogen is attached to carbon, but that carbon is not a carbonyl carbon. In the set we are learning today, this is an amine pattern."

Reveal:

**Amine**

Important boundary:

> "Do not use 'contains nitrogen' as the rule. We still have to check what the nitrogen is directly attached to."

---

### WATCH STEP 7 - Amide

Use **propanamide**.

Highlight in order:
1. the carbonyl `C=O`,
2. the nitrogen,
3. the direct `C(=O)-N` connection.

Dr. Merissa:

> "Here the nitrogen is directly attached to a carbonyl carbon. That one adjacency changes the family."

Reveal:

**Amide**

### Prediction before reveal

Prompt:

**Both molecules contain nitrogen. Which question actually separates the amine from the amide?**

Choices:
- How many total carbons are in the molecule?
- Is the nitrogen directly attached to a carbonyl carbon?
- Is the nitrogen written on the left side of the drawing?

Correct: **Is the nitrogen directly attached to a carbonyl carbon?**

---

## WATCH STEP 8 - Put the six patterns into a relational map

Do not show six isolated flashcards.

Display three contrast pairs:

### Oxygen single-bond family
- alcohol: `C-O-H`
- ether: `C-O-C`

### Carbonyl family
- ketone: carbonyl carbon attached to carbon groups on both sides
- carboxylic acid: `C(=O)-O-H`

### Nitrogen distinction
- amine: N not directly attached to carbonyl C in this introductory set
- amide: `C(=O)-N`

Dr. Merissa:

> "Notice what the names are attached to: not just an element, but a relationship. Oxygen plus hydrogen is not the same as oxygen between two carbons. Nitrogen by itself is not enough. A carbonyl changes what neighboring atoms mean."

---

## WATCH STEP 9 - One molecule can contain more than one functional group

Use **4-aminobutan-2-one**, shown structurally as:
`CH3-C(=O)-CH2-CH2-NH2`

Do not give the name first.

Prompt:

**How many different functional-group patterns from today's set can you find?**

Supported answer:
- ketone,
- amine.

Visual sequence:
1. highlight carbonyl carbon and carbon neighbors -> ketone,
2. separately highlight terminal N not attached to carbonyl C -> amine,
3. show both highlights together.

Dr. Merissa:

> "A molecule does not get only one label because it contains one important region. We scan the whole structure and identify each local pattern that is actually present."

# 6. QUICK CONCEPT CHECK BEFORE BUILD TOGETHER

Supported instruction only.

1. **Every molecule containing O is an alcohol.** False.
2. **C-O-H is the local pattern used here for an alcohol.** True.
3. **C-O-C is the local pattern used here for an ether.** True.
4. **Every carbonyl is automatically a ketone.** False.
5. **A carboxylic acid contains the local pattern C(=O)-O-H.** True.
6. **Every molecule containing N is an amine.** False.
7. **An amide has N directly attached to a carbonyl carbon.** True.
8. **One molecule can contain more than one functional group.** True.
9. **Rotating or redrawing a molecule without changing connectivity changes its functional group.** False.

Any error returns only to the relevant contrast.

1. remove automatic heteroatom halos first,
2. remove the `look for C=O` prompt next,

# 9. MISCONCEPTION CONTRASTS

## M1 - "Contains oxygen means alcohol"

Show fresh pair:
- `CH3CH2OH`,
- `CH3OCH3`.

Representation switch:
Highlight what is directly attached to O.

Dr. Merissa:

> "Both contain oxygen. Only one has O-H. The neighbor relationship decides the functional group."

Repair: learner labels O's two neighbors before naming the group.

---

## M2 - "Any C=O means ketone"

Show:
- `CH3C(=O)CH2CH3`,
- `CH3C(=O)OH`.

Dr. Merissa:

> "The carbonyl is shared by more than one functional group. We have to inspect what else is attached to the carbonyl carbon."

Repair: learner taps the carbonyl carbon's non-oxygen neighbors.

---

## M3 - "Contains nitrogen means amine"

Show:
- `CH3CH2NH2`,
- `CH3C(=O)NH2`.

Dr. Merissa:

> "Both contain nitrogen. The second nitrogen is directly attached to a carbonyl carbon, so it belongs to the amide pattern instead."

Repair: trace N -> directly attached atom -> whether that atom is carbonyl C.

---

## M4 - "Amide is just an amine plus C=O somewhere in the molecule"

Show a molecule with a ketone at one end and an amine several atoms away.

Dr. Merissa:

> "Amide is not 'amine somewhere plus carbonyl somewhere.' The nitrogen must be directly bonded to the carbonyl carbon. Adjacency is the rule."

Repair: learner draws/taps the direct bond required for amide.

---

## M5 - "The whole molecule is the functional group"

Show two different carbon skeletons containing the same alcohol pattern.

Dr. Merissa:

> "The molecule is larger than the functional group. We can change the carbon framework and preserve the same local functional-group pattern."

Repair: highlight only the local atoms/bonds that justify `alcohol`.

---

## M6 - "A molecule can have only one functional group"

Show a fresh structure containing an alcohol and ketone separated by a carbon chain.

Dr. Merissa:

> "Functional groups are local. If two qualifying patterns appear in different regions, the molecule can contain both."

Repair: learner completes a second scan after finding the first group.

---

## M7 - "If the drawing is rotated, the functional group changed"

Show one molecule redrawn in a different orientation with identical connectivity.

Dr. Merissa:

> "Functional-group identity follows connectivity, not page direction. Rotating the drawing does not change who is bonded to whom."

Repair: map the same key atoms between drawings.

---

## M8 - "Carbonyl means the oxygen owns the functional-group name by itself"

Show the same `C=O` fragment embedded once in a ketone and once in a carboxylic acid.

Dr. Merissa:

> "The carbonyl is one shared sub-pattern. The atoms attached around the carbonyl carbon decide the larger functional-group family."

Repair: learner expands the highlighted region one bond outward before naming.

---

## M9 - "Hydrogen attached somewhere near oxygen makes an alcohol"

Show O bonded to two carbons while a nearby carbon bears H labels.

Dr. Merissa:

> "For the alcohol pattern, the hydrogen must be directly bonded to oxygen. Nearby is not enough."

Repair: trace direct O-H bond versus unrelated C-H bond.

## IDK 5 - "I need to see an example"

Use a new supported structure not present in the cold bank. Show one contrast pair only, then return to a fresh item.

## IDK 6 - "This explanation isn't making sense"

Switch to **Pattern-Zoom Mode**:
1. dim the hydrocarbon background,
2. keep O/N and multiple bonds fully visible,
3. draw a one-bond-radius circle around the key atom,
4. label only its directly attached neighbors,
5. display the local pattern in a simplified fragment below,
6. toggle molecule <-> local fragment,
7. ask learner to match the fragment to a named pattern.

If still unclear, use a **neighborhood analogy** only as a bridge:

> "Think of the functional-group name like identifying an intersection by who is directly connected at that corner. Knowing oxygen is in the neighborhood is not enough; we need to know who is directly attached to it."

Immediately map back to real atom connectivity.

Every IDK route returns to a fresh item. The repaired item itself cannot become cold evidence.

## FG-I1 - Alcohol versus ether from exact oxygen connectivity

Use held-out structures:
- **2-butanol**,
- **1-methoxypropane**.

Prompt:

**Classify the oxygen-containing functional group in each molecule and highlight the exact bond pattern that proves your answer.**

### Scoring contract

Required propositions:
1. 2-butanol contains an O directly bonded to H and carbon -> alcohol,
2. 1-methoxypropane contains an O single-bonded between two carbon groups -> ether,
3. oxygen presence alone is not sufficient,
4. the direct neighbors of O must not be role-reversed.

Accept example:
> "The first is an alcohol because the oxygen has an O-H bond. The second is an ether because the oxygen is between two carbons."

Wrong-but-keyword-complete example that must fail:
> "2-butanol is an ether because its oxygen is bonded to hydrogen, while 1-methoxypropane is an alcohol because its oxygen is bonded to two carbons."

Why it fails: it contains all the expected terms but reverses the defining O-neighbor relationships.

---

## FG-I2 - Ketone versus carboxylic acid from carbonyl neighbors

Use held-out structures:
- **3-pentanone**,
- **butanoic acid**.

Prompt:

**Classify each carbonyl-containing functional group and identify what is directly attached to the carbonyl carbon.**

### Scoring contract

Required propositions:
1. both contain C=O,
2. 3-pentanone carbonyl carbon is bonded to carbon groups on both sides -> ketone,
3. butanoic acid carbonyl carbon is directly bonded to O-H -> carboxylic acid,
4. carbonyl presence alone cannot collapse both into ketone.

Accept example:
> "Both have a carbonyl. In 3-pentanone the carbonyl carbon has carbon neighbors on both sides, so it is a ketone. In butanoic acid that carbon is attached to O-H, so it is a carboxylic acid."

Wrong-but-keyword-complete example that must fail:
> "Butanoic acid is the ketone because C=O means ketone, and 3-pentanone is the carboxylic acid because its carbonyl carbon is attached to carbons."

Why it fails: the decisive carbonyl-neighbor roles are reversed.

---

## FG-I3 - Amine versus amide from nitrogen adjacency

Use held-out structures:
- **butan-1-amine**,
- **N-methylbutanamide**.

Prompt:

**Classify the nitrogen-containing functional group in each and explain the single adjacency test that separates them.**

### Scoring contract

Required propositions:
1. butan-1-amine N is not directly attached to a carbonyl carbon -> amine in this introductory set,
2. N-methylbutanamide N is directly attached to carbonyl carbon -> amide,
3. both containing N does not make both amines,
4. `carbonyl somewhere in molecule` is not enough; direct C(=O)-N adjacency is required for amide.

Accept example:
> "The butyl compound is an amine because its N is not attached to a carbonyl carbon. The other is an amide because the N is directly bonded to C=O carbon."

Wrong-but-keyword-complete example that must fail:
> "The first is an amide because it contains nitrogen, and the second is an amine because its nitrogen is attached to the carbonyl carbon."

Why it fails: the amine/amide adjacency rule is reversed.

Second failure mode:
> "Both are amides because both contain nitrogen and both molecules contain carbon atoms."

Why it fails: it replaces direct carbonyl adjacency with irrelevant element presence.

---

## FG-I4 - Find two functional groups in one fresh molecule

Use held-out:
`HO-CH2-CH2-C(=O)-CH3`

Prompt:

**Identify every functional group from the current six-group set and highlight the exact atoms/bonds for each.**

### Scoring contract

Required propositions:
1. terminal `C-O-H` region = alcohol,
2. internal `C(=O)` carbon bonded to carbon groups on both sides = ketone,
3. the two patterns are separate local regions in the same molecule,
4. finding one group does not end the scan,
5. do not merge the distant OH and carbonyl into a carboxylic acid; the OH oxygen is not directly bonded to the carbonyl carbon.

Accept example:
> "There is an alcohol at the HO-CH2 end and a ketone at the separate C=O. It is not a carboxylic acid because the OH is not attached to the carbonyl carbon."

Wrong-but-keyword-complete example that must fail:
> "The molecule is only a carboxylic acid because it contains both an OH and a C=O somewhere in the same structure."

Why it fails: it ignores the required direct C(=O)-O-H connectivity and erases two real separate groups.

---

## FG-I5 - Ether plus amine in one fresh molecule

Use held-out:
`CH3-O-CH2-CH2-CH2-NH2`

Prompt:

**Identify every functional group from the current set and explain why the oxygen and nitrogen receive different labels.**

### Scoring contract

Required propositions:
1. O is single-bonded between two carbons -> ether,
2. N is not directly attached to a carbonyl carbon -> amine,
3. no C=O is present,
4. the O region must not be called alcohol because there is no O-H bond,
5. the N region must not be called amide because there is no direct C(=O)-N pattern.

Accept example:
> "The oxygen is an ether because it sits between two carbons. The nitrogen is an amine because it is not attached to a carbonyl carbon."

Wrong-but-keyword-complete example that must fail:
> "The oxygen is an alcohol because it is bonded to carbon, and the nitrogen is an amide because nitrogen always makes an amide."

Why it fails: both defining relationships are replaced by incomplete element-based shortcuts.

## FG-I6 - Distinguish amide from a separated ketone + amine

Show two held-out structures:

A: `CH3-C(=O)-NH-CH2CH3`

B: `CH3-C(=O)-CH2-CH2-NH-CH3`

Prompt:

**Which structure contains an amide? Which contains a ketone and an amine as two separate groups? Explain using direct attachment.**

### Scoring contract

Required propositions:
1. A has direct `C(=O)-N` adjacency -> amide,
2. B has a carbonyl carbon bonded to carbon groups -> ketone,
3. B's N is separated from the carbonyl by carbon atoms -> amine in this set,
4. `N and C=O both present` is not enough to call B an amide,
5. direct adjacency may not be replaced with visual closeness on the page.

Accept example:
> "A is an amide because N is bonded directly to the carbonyl carbon. B has a ketone and a separate amine because the N is not directly attached to that carbonyl carbon."

Wrong-but-keyword-complete example that must fail:
> "B is the amide because the nitrogen is drawn near the C=O, while A is a ketone and amine because the N comes after the carbonyl."

Why it fails: it substitutes page position for bond connectivity and reverses the actual adjacency.

## FG-I7 - Reverse task: highlight evidence for named groups

Use held-out molecule:
`HO-CH2-CH2-CH2-NH-CH2CH3`

Prompt:

**This molecule is said to contain an alcohol and an amine. Highlight the minimum atoms/bonds that prove each label and explain why there is no amide.**

### Scoring contract

Required propositions:
1. alcohol evidence includes O-H with O bonded to carbon,
2. amine evidence includes N attached to carbon groups without direct carbonyl-C attachment,
3. no C=O exists in the structure, so amide pattern is absent,
4. highlighting an unrelated C-H bond as alcohol evidence fails,
5. nitrogen presence alone is insufficient explanation for amine.

Accept example:
> "The O-H at the left proves alcohol. The nitrogen is bonded to carbons and there is no carbonyl carbon attached to it, so it is an amine, not an amide."

Wrong-but-keyword-complete example that must fail:
> "The alcohol is proven by a C-H bond near oxygen, and the amide is proven by nitrogen even though there is no C=O."

Why it fails: both evidence relationships are wrong despite using the expected atom labels.

---

## FG-I8 - Mixed classification under redrawing

Show four held-out molecule pairs. In each pair, the second drawing is a rotated/redrawn version of the first with identical connectivity. Use one representative each of:
- alcohol,
- ether,
- ketone,
- amide.

Prompt:

**For each pair, decide whether the functional-group identity stayed the same after redrawing. Then identify the local pattern that proves it.**

### Scoring contract

A clean success requires:
1. identical connectivity across each redraw is recognized,
2. functional-group identity is preserved under page rotation/redrawing,
3. each classification is justified by the correct local atom/bond relationship,
4. visual left/right/up/down position is not accepted as chemistry,
5. a correct label paired with a role-reversed structural explanation does not count as explanation evidence.

Accept explanation shape:
> "The amide stays an amide after the drawing rotates because the nitrogen is still directly bonded to the same carbonyl carbon. The page direction changed, not the connectivity."

Wrong-but-keyword-complete example that must fail:
> "The amide becomes an amine when the nitrogen moves to the left side of the page, even though the C(=O)-N bond is unchanged."

Why it fails: it treats drawing orientation as a connectivity change.

Contradiction rule:
A response that first says `connectivity is unchanged` and later claims `the functional group changed because the drawing rotated` does not pass explanation evidence.

---

# 12. INDEPENDENT EVIDENCE RULE

Independent evidence requires a cold fresh item completed without support.

Do not display `Mastered` after one correct label.

To satisfy shared mastery, the engine must eventually record:

1. at least one cold item requiring exact pattern discrimination,
2. a role-preserving explanation that cites the decisive atom/bond relationship,
3. a second different cold success after a meaningful interval.

Recommended coverage before mixed Test 1 practice:
- one oxygen-pair contrast,
- one carbonyl-pair contrast,
- one amine/amide contrast,
- one multi-functional molecule,
- later fresh retrieval.

Objective correctness and explanation correctness are scored separately.

A lucky correct label with a reversed pattern explanation does not create explanation evidence.

A correct explanation attached to the wrong highlighted atoms/bonds is not a clean independent success.

After support or correction, explanation evidence must be recollected on a fresh prompt rather than by asking the learner to repeat the corrected sentence.

# 13. EXPLAIN-WHY PROMPTS - ROLE-PRESERVING SCORING

## E-W1 - Why is an alcohol not identified by oxygen alone?

Required propositions:
1. multiple functional groups contain oxygen,
2. alcohol in this set requires an O-H bond with O attached to carbon,
3. ether uses O between carbon groups instead,
4. therefore direct connectivity around O determines the label.

Accept:
> "Oxygen alone is not enough because ethers also contain oxygen. An alcohol has O-H, while an ether has O between carbons."

Wrong-but-keyword-complete failure:
> "Alcohol and ether are the same because both contain oxygen; whether oxygen bonds to H or C does not matter."

Why it fails: it erases the deciding neighbor relationship.

---

## E-W2 - Why is every carbonyl not a ketone?

Required propositions:
1. carbonyl means C=O,
2. more than one functional group contains that sub-pattern,
3. ketone requires carbon groups attached to the carbonyl carbon on both non-oxygen sides in the examples here,
4. carboxylic acid instead contains C(=O)-O-H.

Accept:
> "C=O tells me there is a carbonyl, but I still inspect the carbonyl carbon's neighbors. Carbon on both sides gives the ketone pattern; O-H attached to that carbon gives carboxylic acid."

Wrong-but-keyword-complete failure:
> "Every C=O is a ketone, and a carboxylic acid is identified only by having oxygen somewhere else."

Why it fails: it replaces the larger connectivity pattern with carbonyl presence alone.

---

## E-W3 - Why is an amide not the same as an amine?

Required propositions:
1. both may contain N,
2. in an amide the N is directly attached to a carbonyl carbon,
3. in the introductory amine examples the N is not directly attached to carbonyl C,
4. direct adjacency is the deciding relationship.

Accept:
> "The difference is what the nitrogen is bonded to. If N is directly attached to the carbonyl carbon, that region is an amide."

Wrong-but-keyword-complete failure:
> "Amide and amine are separated by whether nitrogen is written before or after the carbonyl on the page."

Why it fails: page order replaces bond connectivity.

---

## E-W4 - Why can one molecule contain more than one functional group?

Required propositions:
1. functional groups are local patterns,
2. different qualifying patterns can occur in different regions of the same molecule,
3. finding one group does not invalidate another,
4. the molecule must be rescanned.

Accept:
> "A functional group is local, so one part of the molecule can contain an alcohol pattern while another part contains a ketone pattern."

Wrong-but-keyword-complete failure:
> "A molecule can only have one functional group because the first group found names the entire molecule and cancels the others."

Why it fails: it treats a local pattern as mutually exclusive whole-molecule identity.

---

## E-W5 - Why does redrawing not change the functional group?

Required propositions:
1. functional-group identity depends on atom connectivity and bond order,
2. rotating/redrawing without breaking/reconnecting bonds preserves that connectivity,
3. therefore the functional-group identity remains the same.

Accept:
> "Turning the drawing does not change who is bonded to whom, so the same local pattern is still present."

Wrong-but-keyword-complete failure:
> "The functional group changes when the atoms move to a different side of the page even if the bonds stay the same."

Why it fails: visual orientation is incorrectly treated as chemical connectivity.

# 21. DEFINITION OF DONE FOR U1-03

The lesson is successful when a beginner can look at an unfamiliar molecule and say, in her own reasoning:

- where the relevant O/N atoms are,
- whether C=O is present,
- what atoms are directly attached to the key atom/carbonyl carbon,
- whether the local pattern is alcohol, ether, ketone, carboxylic acid, amine, or amide,
- whether more than one qualifying pattern is present,
- which exact atoms/bonds justify each label,
- and why a tempting near-lookalike label is wrong,

then complete a fresh cold identification without the teaching screen.

The standard is not:

> "She memorized six pictures."

The standard is:

> **"She can isolate the local pattern, preserve the bond relationships, and name the functional group from connectivity."**
