**Purpose of this file:** teach intermolecular-force and boiling-point reasoning as a causal chain from molecular structure to attractions between separate molecules to the energy required for molecules to separate into the gas phase. The learner must distinguish intermolecular attractions from covalent bonds inside a molecule, identify all relevant intermolecular-force classes in the introductory scope, recognize hydrogen-bond donor/acceptor requirements rather than using element-presence shortcuts, and compare boiling points by weighing both attraction type and dispersion/shape evidence.

Cold independent items in this file carry their own role-preserving scoring contracts from the start. Exact cold molecules are named in Section 11 so the implementation cannot silently substitute a recently taught example.

# 1. LESSON GOAL

A learner who enters with usable U1-03 functional-group recognition but little or no usable intermolecular-force knowledge should leave able to:

1. distinguish a covalent bond **inside** a molecule from an intermolecular attraction **between separate molecules**,
2. explain that boiling separates molecules from one another rather than breaking the ordinary covalent framework of each molecule,
3. recognize that **London dispersion forces** are present between all molecules,
4. explain dispersion qualitatively as attraction associated with temporary/induced uneven electron distributions,
5. recognize that larger/more polarizable electron clouds generally strengthen dispersion when other factors are comparable,
6. recognize that molecular shape/contact area can change dispersion among comparable molecules,
7. recognize **dipole-dipole attraction** between polar molecules,
8. understand that a polar molecule still also has London dispersion,
9. identify a hydrogen-bond **donor** in the current scope by a suitable `O-H` or `N-H` bond,
10. identify a hydrogen-bond **acceptor** in the current scope by a suitable available lone-pair site, especially oxygen or nitrogen patterns already established in U1-03,
11. explain that self-hydrogen-bonding in a pure substance requires molecules that can supply a donor and an acceptor relationship,
12. reject `contains O = hydrogen bonds` and `contains N = hydrogen bonds` shortcuts,
13. distinguish `all intermolecular forces present` from `the strongest/dominant structural feature in this comparison`,
14. predict that stronger intermolecular attractions generally raise boiling point when comparing otherwise similar molecules,
15. use molecular size/polarizability and shape when hydrogen bonding or polarity alone does not settle a boiling-point comparison,
16. reject `higher molar mass always wins` and `larger ring/chain always boils higher` as universal rules,
17. rank a fresh small set qualitatively and justify the ranking from structure rather than a memorized force hierarchy.

This lesson is not complete because the learner can chant:

> `hydrogen bonding > dipole-dipole > dispersion`.

That shortcut fails whenever size, shape, donor availability, or competing structural features matter.

The lesson is complete when the learner can inspect the structures, identify what attractions are actually possible, decide which structural differences matter in the comparison, and explain the predicted boiling-point direction.

# 2. LEARNER-FACING ORIENTATION

## Screen title

**Intermolecular forces: what makes molecules hold onto each other?**

## Dr. Merissa narration

> "The bonds inside one molecule hold its atoms together. Intermolecular forces are different. They are attractions between separate molecules. When a liquid boils, the molecules have to pull away from one another, so stronger attractions usually mean more energy is needed before the liquid can become a gas."

### Visual

Show two ethanol molecules separated by clear space.

- solid lines inside each molecule = covalent bonds,
- one unlabeled dashed line between the molecules,
- no force name yet.

### Immediate learner check

Prompt:

**Which line is between two separate molecules: the solid O-H bond inside one molecule or the dashed connection between the molecules?**

Correct: **the dashed connection between the molecules**

Then ask:

**When this liquid boils, do we normally break every C-C, C-O, and O-H covalent bond, or do the molecules separate from one another?**

Correct: **the molecules separate from one another**

If missed:

> "Boiling is a physical change. The ethanol molecules move apart into the gas phase; we are not tearing each ethanol molecule into atoms."

Orientation is instructional only, not mastery evidence.

# 4. TEACH THE QUESTION TYPES BEFORE THE FORCE NAMES STACK UP

Learners commonly answer the wrong question correctly. Establish two separate jobs.

## Question type A - What forces are present?

Dr. Merissa:

> "One question may ask for **all** intermolecular forces present. A polar molecule does not lose its dispersion forces just because it also has a dipole."

## Question type B - What difference best explains a boiling-point comparison?

Dr. Merissa:

> "A different question may ask which structural difference matters most for a comparison. Then we identify what changed between the molecules instead of listing every force and stopping."

### Interaction

Prompt:

**If a molecule has hydrogen bonding, should we erase London dispersion from its list of forces?**

Correct: **No**

# 5. TEACHING SEQUENCE - I DO

## WATCH STEP 1 - London dispersion begins with moving electrons

### Worked system

Use two **hexane** molecules.

### Visual

1. Show two neutral hexane molecules with electron-cloud halos.
2. Freeze one moment where the electron cloud is temporarily a little denser on one side of molecule A.
3. Mark that side `temporary partial -` and the opposite side `temporary partial +`.
4. Show molecule B's cloud responding so the nearby side becomes temporarily complementary.
5. Add a subtle dashed attraction between the temporary opposite regions.

### Dr. Merissa

> "Electrons are always moving. At one instant, the electron cloud can be a little more concentrated on one side of a molecule. That temporary unevenness can induce a temporary unevenness in a nearby molecule. The resulting attraction is called a **London dispersion force**."

Then:

> "Dispersion is not a special feature of nonpolar molecules. It occurs between **all** molecules. In nonpolar hydrocarbons, it may be the main intermolecular attraction we need to consider."

### Prediction

Prompt:

**Does ethanol still have London dispersion even though it also has oxygen and other attractions?**

Correct: **Yes**

If learner says no:

> "Every electron cloud can fluctuate. Additional force types can be added, but dispersion does not disappear."

---

## WATCH STEP 2 - Why size can strengthen dispersion

### Visual

Compare a short hydrocarbon and a longer straight-chain hydrocarbon using generic labels rather than a cold molecule.

Show:
- smaller electron cloud,
- larger electron cloud with more electrons and easier temporary distortion,
- larger contact region when the chains lie alongside one another.

### Dr. Merissa

> "When molecules are otherwise comparable, a larger, more polarizable electron cloud usually produces stronger dispersion. More electrons and a larger cloud make temporary unevenness easier to create. Shape also matters because molecules that can contact one another over more surface can accumulate more dispersion attraction."

Important guardrail:

> "Molar mass can be a useful clue in a family of similar molecules, but molar mass is not the force itself and it is not an automatic winner over every other structural feature."

### Interaction

Prompt:

**Which is the better explanation: 'the number on the molar-mass label pulls harder,' or 'the larger, more polarizable electron cloud can create stronger dispersion'?**

Correct: **larger/more polarizable electron cloud**

---

## WATCH STEP 3 - Dipole-dipole attraction requires persistent polarity

### Worked system

Use two **propanone (acetone)** molecules.

### Visual

Highlight each carbonyl:
- oxygen side = persistent partial negative,
- carbonyl-carbon side = persistent partial positive.

Bring the molecules near each other and rotate them so opposite partial-charge regions approach.

### Dr. Merissa

> "A polar molecule has a persistent uneven charge distribution. When the partially positive region of one polar molecule attracts the partially negative region of another, that is **dipole-dipole attraction**."

Then immediately preserve accumulation:

> "Acetone still has dispersion too. The dipole-dipole attraction is an additional intermolecular attraction, not a replacement."

### Interaction

Prompt:

**For acetone, which list is more complete?**
- dipole-dipole only
- London dispersion only
- London dispersion + dipole-dipole

Correct: **London dispersion + dipole-dipole**

---

## WATCH STEP 4 - Build hydrogen bonding from donor and acceptor roles

### Worked system

Use two **ethanol** molecules.

Do not begin with the term `hydrogen bond`.

### Visual A - donor

Highlight ethanol's O-H bond.

Dr. Merissa:

> "This hydrogen is directly bonded to oxygen. In the group patterns we are using in Unit 1, an O-H or suitable N-H bond can provide a strongly partial-positive hydrogen that participates in a special intermolecular attraction. The molecule providing that O-H or N-H hydrogen is called a **hydrogen-bond donor**."

### Interaction

Prompt:

**In ethanol, which atom is directly bonded to the donor hydrogen?**

Correct: **oxygen**

### Visual B - acceptor

Highlight the oxygen lone-pair region on the second ethanol molecule.

Dr. Merissa:

> "The partner molecule needs a suitable electron-rich lone-pair site. In our current structures, oxygen and some nitrogen sites commonly play this role. The molecule providing that site is a **hydrogen-bond acceptor**."

### Interaction

Prompt:

**For this ethanol pair, does the dashed intermolecular attraction run from the donor O-H hydrogen toward the other molecule's oxygen region, or from carbon to carbon?**

Correct: **from the donor O-H hydrogen toward the other oxygen**

### Reveal the term

Add dashed `O-H...O` intermolecular line.

Dr. Merissa:

> "That donor-to-acceptor attraction is called **hydrogen bonding**. It is an intermolecular attraction. The solid O-H covalent bond inside ethanol stays intact."

---

## WATCH STEP 5 - Hydrogen bonding is not `contains oxygen`

### Contrast

Show:
- ethanol `CH3CH2OH`,
- dimethyl ether `CH3OCH3`.

Both have oxygen.

### Visual

For ethanol:
- O-H donor present,
- O lone-pair acceptor present,
- self-hydrogen-bonding between ethanol molecules possible.

For dimethyl ether:
- oxygen acceptor present,
- **no O-H or N-H donor**,
- two pure dimethyl-ether molecules cannot donate a hydrogen bond to one another.

### Dr. Merissa

> "Both molecules contain oxygen. Only ethanol has an O-H donor. Dimethyl ether can accept a hydrogen bond from a different molecule that supplies an O-H or N-H donor, but a pure sample of dimethyl ether does not have its own O-H or N-H donor to create ether-to-ether hydrogen bonding."

### Interaction

Prompt:

**Which statement is correct?**
- Any molecule with oxygen hydrogen-bonds to itself
- A self-hydrogen-bonding pair needs an appropriate donor and acceptor relationship

Correct: **appropriate donor and acceptor relationship**

---

## WATCH STEP 6 - Hydrogen bonding is not `contains nitrogen`

### Contrast

Show:
- **ethylamine** `CH3CH2NH2`,
- **triethylamine** `N(CH2CH3)3`.

### Dr. Merissa

> "Nitrogen creates the same kind of shortcut trap. Ethylamine has N-H bonds, so one ethylamine molecule can donate through N-H while another nitrogen can accept. Triethylamine is polar and its nitrogen can accept a hydrogen bond from another donor, but triethylamine has no N-H bond, so two triethylamine molecules cannot donate hydrogen bonds to each other."

Scope note:

> "We are learning donor/acceptor logic, not memorizing 'amine equals hydrogen bonding.' Functional-group details decide whether a donor is actually present."

### Interaction

Prompt:

**Which molecule supplies an N-H donor in this pair?**

Correct: **ethylamine**

## WATCH STEP 7 - Carboxylic acid and amide connection from U1-03

Do not reteach functional-group recognition. Reuse the exact local-pattern logic.

### Visual

Show small supported examples:
- **ethanoic acid** with `C(=O)-O-H` highlighted,
- **acetamide** with `C(=O)-NH2` highlighted.

### Dr. Merissa

> "U1-03 taught you to inspect direct connectivity. That same habit matters here. A carboxylic acid contains an O-H donor and a carbonyl oxygen that can accept. A primary amide contains N-H donor bonds and a carbonyl oxygen acceptor. We do not decide by seeing O or N somewhere on the page; we inspect the actual bonds."

Do not require a complete hydrogen-bond acceptor table for all later functional groups in this lesson.

### Interaction

Prompt:

**In acetamide, which visible bond supplies the donor hydrogen: C=O or N-H?**

## WATCH STEP 8 - Connect attractions to boiling without breaking molecules

### Visual

Show a liquid box with many intact molecules.

Sequence:
1. low temperature: molecules close together,
2. add heat/energy,
3. molecules move faster,
4. at boiling, enough molecules separate into gas while each molecular framework remains intact.

### Dr. Merissa

> "Boiling requires molecules to overcome the attractions holding separate molecules near one another. If those attractions are stronger, more thermal energy is generally required to separate the molecules, so the boiling point tends to be higher."

### Prediction

Prompt:

**If two otherwise similar liquids differ mainly because one can form strong self-hydrogen bonds and the other cannot, which one would you generally expect to require more energy to boil?**

Correct: **the self-hydrogen-bonding liquid**

Guardrail:

> "That is an otherwise-similar comparison. We still check size and shape before turning it into a universal rule."

---

## WATCH STEP 9 - Compare similar-size oxygen isomers

### Worked comparison

Use:
- **1-butanol** `CH3CH2CH2CH2OH`,
- **ethoxyethane (diethyl ether)** `CH3CH2OCH2CH3`.

Both have formula `C4H10O` and the same molar mass.

### Dr. Merissa

> "This is a clean comparison because the molecular formulas and molar masses match. Both have dispersion. Both have polar C-O regions. But 1-butanol has an O-H donor and can form strong alcohol-to-alcohol hydrogen bonds. Ethoxyethane has no O-H or N-H donor, so it cannot self-hydrogen-bond in the pure liquid."

### Prediction

Prompt:

**Which should have the higher boiling point: 1-butanol or ethoxyethane?**

Correct: **1-butanol**

Reason required in supported form:
- stronger intermolecular attraction from self-hydrogen bonding in 1-butanol,
- not `because alcohols always boil higher` as a memorized family slogan.

---

## WATCH STEP 10 - Shape can matter even when force class is the same

### Worked comparison

Use two C6 alkane isomers:
- **n-hexane**,
- **2,2-dimethylbutane**.

Both are nonpolar hydrocarbons with the same molecular formula and molar mass.

### Visual

Show equal electron-count badge, then place molecules against a flat contact surface:
- extended n-hexane can make broader contact,
- compact 2,2-dimethylbutane has less effective surface contact.

### Dr. Merissa

> "These molecules have the same formula and the same main force class: London dispersion. The straighter molecule can make more surface contact with neighboring molecules, which generally strengthens total dispersion interactions. The more compact branched isomer has less contact and therefore a lower boiling point."

### Interaction

Prompt:

**Did branching create a new force type, or did it change how effectively the molecules can contact one another?**

Correct: **it changed molecular contact/dispersion strength**

Guardrail:

> "Use this shape rule for genuinely comparable isomers. Do not turn 'less branched = always higher boiling point' into a universal law across unrelated functional groups."

---

## WATCH STEP 11 - Use a decision order instead of a force hierarchy chant

### Decision sequence visual

Show one card at a time:

1. **What molecules am I comparing?**
2. **What intermolecular forces are actually present in each?**
3. **Is there an O-H/N-H donor and a suitable acceptor for self-hydrogen bonding?**
4. **Are the molecules otherwise similar in size/polarizability?**
5. **If force classes are similar, does shape/contact area differ?**
6. **Which structural difference best explains the predicted boiling-point direction?**

### Dr. Merissa

> "The point is not to memorize a single ladder. We compare the real molecules. Hydrogen bonding can dominate a close comparison, but a much larger nonpolar molecule can have very strong dispersion. Size and shape matter. The question is always: what changed, and how does that change the attractions between molecules?"

# 6. QUICK CONCEPT CHECK BEFORE BUILD TOGETHER

Supported instruction only.

1. **London dispersion occurs only in nonpolar molecules.** False.
2. **A polar molecule can have both dispersion and dipole-dipole attraction.** True.
3. **The covalent O-H bond inside ethanol is itself the intermolecular hydrogen bond.** False.
4. **An ether oxygen can accept a hydrogen bond even though a pure ether sample has no O-H donor.** True.
5. **Any molecule containing nitrogen automatically self-hydrogen-bonds.** False.
6. **Boiling normally requires breaking the molecule's C-C covalent framework.** False.
7. **Stronger attractions generally raise boiling point when other structural factors are comparable.** True.
8. **Among comparable nonpolar isomers, more compact branching can reduce surface contact and lower boiling point.** True.
9. **Molar mass alone is a universal boiling-point rule.** False.
10. **When asked for all forces present, hydrogen bonding does not erase dispersion.** True.

Any error returns only to the relevant representation.

# 9. MISCONCEPTION CONTRASTS

## M1 - "London dispersion only happens in nonpolar molecules"

### Representation switch

Show ethanol with three toggles:
- dispersion ON,
- dipole-dipole ON,
- hydrogen bonding ON.

Dr. Merissa:

> "Additional attractions stack on top of dispersion. The presence of a stronger or more noticeable force does not delete the fluctuating electron cloud."

Repair: classify two polar molecules as `dispersion also present` or `dispersion absent`.

---

## M2 - "Boiling breaks the covalent bonds inside the molecule"

### Representation switch

Show intact molecule cards moving farther apart while every internal solid bond remains unchanged.

Dr. Merissa:

> "Boiling separates molecules from one another. It does not normally decompose the molecule by breaking its ordinary covalent skeleton."

Repair: tap the line that is being overcome during boiling: intermolecular dashed attraction, not internal solid bond.

---

## M3 - "Any O means hydrogen bonding"

### Representation switch

Compare ethanol with dimethyl ether.

Both O atoms highlighted.

Then highlight only ethanol's O-H donor.

Dr. Merissa:

> "An acceptor is not automatically a donor. For molecules to hydrogen-bond to themselves, one molecule must supply an appropriate donor hydrogen and another must supply an acceptor site."

Repair: identify donor, acceptor, or both for a fresh pair.

---

## M4 - "Any N means hydrogen bonding"

### Representation switch

Compare ethylamine with triethylamine.

Dr. Merissa:

> "The nitrogen label alone is not enough. Ask whether an N-H donor is actually present. A tertiary amine can accept hydrogen bonds from another donor but has no N-H hydrogen to donate."

Repair: trace the bond directly from N to H before assigning donor status.

---

## M5 - "Hydrogen bonding replaces dipole-dipole and dispersion"

### Representation switch

Build a layered force stack for ethanol:
1. dispersion,
2. polarity/dipole-dipole,
3. hydrogen bonding.

Dr. Merissa:

> "When a question asks for all intermolecular forces present, we keep every applicable layer. Hydrogen bonding is a particularly strong directional polar interaction, but course-style force lists still record the applicable categories rather than erasing the others."

Repair: select all forces for a fresh alcohol.

---

## M6 - "The heaviest molecule always has the highest boiling point"

### Representation switch

Show two abstract molecules:
- one somewhat heavier but nonpolar/compact,
- one similar-size molecule with a strong donor-acceptor network.

Do not reveal a numeric boiling point.

Dr. Merissa:

> "Molar mass often tracks dispersion within a similar family, but it is not a universal ranking rule. We must inspect the actual intermolecular attractions and shape."

Repair: state what structural information is still missing before making a ranking.

---

## M7 - "If both molecules have London dispersion, their boiling points must match"

### Representation switch

Show n-hexane and 2,2-dimethylbutane with identical formula badges but different contact silhouettes.

Dr. Merissa:

> "Same force category does not mean same force strength. Electron-cloud size and molecular contact geometry can change the total dispersion attraction."

Repair: identify which isomer can make broader contact.

---

## M8 - "More branching always raises boiling point because there are more branches touching"

### Representation switch

Compress a straight-chain alkane into a more compact branched silhouette while preserving formula.

Dr. Merissa:

> "For comparable alkane isomers, branching usually makes the molecule more compact and reduces effective surface contact. Less contact generally lowers boiling point."

Repair: choose broader versus more compact contact shape.

---

## M9 - "O-H on the page means the dashed hydrogen bond is the same O-H bond"

### Representation switch

Use two different line styles and molecule outlines:
- solid `O-H` inside molecule A,
- dashed `H...O` from molecule A to molecule B.

Dr. Merissa:

> "The solid O-H covalent bond creates the donor. The dashed attraction reaches from that donor hydrogen to a different molecule. They are not the same bond."

Repair: learner traces the donor molecule boundary before placing the dashed attraction.

---

## M10 - "A molecule that cannot self-hydrogen-bond can never participate in hydrogen bonding"

### Representation switch

Dr. Merissa:

> "Dimethyl ether has no O-H donor, so ether molecules cannot donate to each other. But its oxygen can accept a hydrogen bond from methanol's O-H donor. Self-hydrogen-bonding and hydrogen-bond participation are different questions."

Repair: label each partner `donor`, `acceptor`, or `both`.

## M11 - "A force ladder answers every boiling-point question"

## IMF-I1 - Intramolecular versus intermolecular in 2-butanol

Use two fresh **2-butanol** molecules.

Prompt:

**Mark one covalent O-H bond inside a molecule, then mark one possible hydrogen-bond attraction between the two molecules. Explain why the two marks represent different kinds of interactions.**

### Scoring contract

Required propositions:
1. the covalent O-H bond is inside one 2-butanol molecule,
2. the intermolecular hydrogen bond runs from an O-H donor hydrogen on one molecule toward an oxygen acceptor on another molecule,
3. the intermolecular attraction does not replace or break the donor's covalent O-H bond,
4. boiling separates molecules by overcoming intermolecular attractions rather than treating the internal O-H bond as the force between molecules.

Accept example:
> "The solid O-H bond is inside one 2-butanol molecule. The hydrogen bond is a dashed attraction from that O-H hydrogen to the oxygen on another 2-butanol molecule."

Wrong-but-keyword-complete example that must fail:
> "The O-H covalent bond is the hydrogen bond between the two molecules, so boiling breaks that O-H bond and sends the hydrogen to the second oxygen."

Why it fails: it collapses intramolecular and intermolecular roles and turns boiling into bond cleavage.

## IMF-I2 - Identify all forces in pure 2-pentanone

Use **2-pentanone**.

Prompt:

**Select every intermolecular-force class present between molecules in a pure sample of 2-pentanone, then explain why self-hydrogen-bonding is or is not included.**

### Scoring contract

2. the polar carbonyl makes dipole-dipole attraction present,
3. 2-pentanone has a carbonyl oxygen that can accept hydrogen bonds from another donor substance,

# 13. EXPLAIN-WHY PROMPTS - ROLE-PRESERVING SCORING

## E-W1 - Why is London dispersion present in all molecules?

Required propositions:
1. all molecules contain moving electrons/electron clouds,
2. temporary uneven electron distribution can occur,
3. that temporary unevenness can induce/respond to unevenness in a nearby molecule,
4. the resulting attraction is dispersion,
5. polarity or hydrogen bonding does not turn dispersion off.

Accept:
> "Every molecule has a moving electron cloud that can become temporarily uneven and induce a temporary dipole nearby, so dispersion is present even in polar molecules."

Wrong-but-keyword-complete failure:
> "Dispersion is present only when a molecule has no dipole; once dipole-dipole or hydrogen bonding appears, the electron cloud stops creating dispersion."

Why it fails: it reverses the cumulative-force relationship.

---

## E-W2 - Why does pure ether not self-hydrogen-bond even though oxygen has lone pairs?

Required propositions:
1. ether oxygen can act as an acceptor,
2. the ether has no O-H or N-H donor,
3. self-hydrogen-bonding requires donor and acceptor partners,
4. therefore pure ether molecules cannot donate to one another,
5. the ether can still accept a hydrogen bond from another donor-containing molecule.

Accept:
> "The ether oxygen can accept, but the ether has no O-H or N-H donor. Two ether molecules therefore cannot self-hydrogen-bond, although an alcohol could donate to the ether oxygen."

Wrong-but-keyword-complete failure:
> "The ether self-hydrogen-bonds because any oxygen lone pair is both the donor and acceptor; no O-H bond is needed."

Why it fails: donor and acceptor roles are collapsed.

---

## E-W3 - Why does stronger intermolecular attraction usually raise boiling point in a close comparison?

Required propositions:
1. boiling requires molecules to separate from neighbors into the gas phase,
2. stronger attractions between molecules require more energy to overcome,
3. more thermal energy corresponds to a higher boiling temperature under comparable conditions,
4. ordinary covalent skeleton bonds are not what is being broken in normal boiling.

Accept:
> "Stronger intermolecular attractions take more energy to overcome before molecules can separate into the gas, so the boiling point is higher when the other factors are comparable."

Wrong-but-keyword-complete failure:
> "Stronger attractions lower boiling point because they help molecules escape, and boiling happens by breaking the covalent bonds inside each molecule."

Why it fails: both energy direction and interaction type are reversed.

---

## E-W4 - Why can straight-chain and highly branched isomers have different boiling points?

Required propositions:
1. same formula/mass does not guarantee identical intermolecular interaction strength,
2. among comparable nonpolar isomers, shape changes effective surface contact,
3. less-branched/extended shapes can make more contact,
4. more contact strengthens accumulated dispersion,
5. compact branching generally lowers boiling point in the comparison.

Accept:
> "The isomers have the same mass, but the straighter shape can contact neighboring molecules over more surface, strengthening dispersion and raising boiling point relative to the compact branched isomer."

Wrong-but-keyword-complete failure:
> "Branching raises boiling point because every branch adds a new London-dispersion force type, while straight chains have less surface contact."

Why it fails: branching does not create a new force class and the contact relationship is reversed.

---

## E-W5 - Why is `hydrogen bonding > dipole > dispersion` not enough by itself to rank every pair?

Required propositions:
1. all molecules have dispersion,
2. dispersion strength changes with electron-cloud size/polarizability and shape/contact,
3. donor/acceptor availability determines whether self-hydrogen-bonding is actually possible,
4. molecule size and shape can differ greatly across unrelated structures,
5. therefore the actual structures must be compared rather than applying an absolute force ladder blindly.

Accept:
> "The force labels are useful, but their strengths depend on the actual molecules. A large, polarizable molecule can have strong dispersion, and a molecule with oxygen may not even have a hydrogen-bond donor. I still have to compare structure, size, and donor/acceptor patterns."

Wrong-but-keyword-complete failure:
> "The force ladder is universal: any molecule with hydrogen bonding must boil above every molecule with only dispersion, regardless of size or structure."

Why it fails: it turns a teaching heuristic into a universal law.

# 21. DEFINITION OF DONE FOR U1-04

The lesson is successful when a beginner can look at fresh structures and say, in her own reasoning:

- which lines are covalent bonds inside a molecule and which interactions are between molecules,
- why London dispersion is present,
- whether dipole-dipole attraction is present,
- whether a real O-H/N-H donor exists,
- whether a suitable acceptor exists,
- whether the substance can self-hydrogen-bond or only participate with a different donor-containing molecule,
- which intermolecular forces are present in total,
- whether size/polarizability or shape/contact changes dispersion,
- which structural difference is most relevant to a boiling-point comparison,
- and why stronger intermolecular attraction generally raises boiling point in a close comparison,

then complete a fresh cold force-identification and boiling-point task without the teaching screen.

The standard is not:

> "She memorized hydrogen bonding > dipole-dipole > London dispersion."

The standard is:

> **"She can inspect the actual molecules, identify what attractions are really possible, and use those attractions plus size and shape to explain which liquid should be harder to separate into the gas phase."**
